## Clean VO text for TTS

```
Click.

You put your headphones on so nobody talks to you.

But your open desk wasn't designed for talking.

It started in nineteen-oh-six. In a building where every desk could be seen from above.

And the man who later tried to give you walls... lived to regret it.

Buffalo, New York. The Larkin Company sells soap by mail.

Thousands of orders a day. Hundreds of clerks.

They hire a young architect named Frank Lloyd Wright.

He builds them a cathedral of paperwork.

One great open hall. Light pouring from the roof.

Beautiful. And from the balconies above, every desk — visible.

Every clerk — in sight. Every pause — noticed.

He even designed the furniture. The chairs were attached to the desks.

A decade later, the stopwatch walked into the office too.

In nineteen seventeen, William Leffingwell put Taylor's methods on the clerk's desk.

Desks in rows. Work flowing in a line. The supervisor where he could see it all.

The factory floor had simply moved upstairs.

Then, in the late nineteen-fifties, two brothers in Germany tried to fix it.

They called it the office landscape.

No rows. No corner offices. Desks in loose clusters, plants between them.

Boss and clerk on the same floor.

It was meant to be human. Companies kept the open floor... and dropped the rest.

Nineteen sixty-eight. A designer named Robert Propst, at Herman Miller, launches the Action Office.

His idea: give every worker their own adjustable space.

Panels. Privacy. Freedom to move.

Companies took his panels... and packed them into boxes.

Smaller. Cheaper. Endless. The cubicle was born.

Near the end of his life, Propst called it "monolithic insanity."

So the walls came down again. This time, they called it collaboration.

In twenty eighteen, two Harvard researchers tracked people moving from cubicles into an open office.

Face-to-face conversation didn't go up. It dropped — by about seventy percent.

People emailed and messaged instead. And everyone put their headphones on.

A hundred and twenty years. Rows, landscapes, cubicles, open plan.

Different shapes. The same balcony.

You're not antisocial. You're not difficult.

You're just building the wall they took away.

Click.
```

## Fact-check before recording

- [ ] Larkin Administration Building, Buffalo, Frank Lloyd Wright, completed 1906; central light
      court with balconies; steel furniture with chairs attached to desks.
- [ ] Larkin: soap by mail; "thousands of orders a day, hundreds of clerks."
- [ ] William Leffingwell, *Scientific Office Management* (1917).
- [ ] Quickborner Team (Eberhard and Wolfgang Schnelle), Bürolandschaft, late 1950s.
- [ ] Robert Propst, Herman Miller, Action Office (II), 1968.
- [ ] Propst: "monolithic insanity" (late 1990s; he died in 2000).
- [ ] Bernstein & Turban (2018), Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B: face-to-face interaction fell ~70% after
      a move from cubicles to open-plan; email and messaging rose.
- [ ] "A hundred and twenty years" = 1906 to 2026.
