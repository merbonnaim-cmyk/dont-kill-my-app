# Video 2 — The open office

Working title: `The Open Office Was Never Built For You`
Format: narrated, illustrated, ~2.5-3 min. Same style as video 1.

## Hooks (top two of five, scored with hookscore.py)

**A — 71 WORKABLE (The Mistake).** Recommended: fits the voice, keeps the opening sound.
> Click. You put your headphones on so nobody talks to you. But your open desk was designed in
> nineteen-oh-six, so you'd never stop being seen.

**B — 73 STRONG (Contrarian Flip).**
> You wear headphones so nobody interrupts you. Nobody tells you the open office started in
> nineteen-oh-six, and you lose the walls before you lose anything else.

Both are weakest on SPECIFICITY. Tool advice: swap an adjective for a number, name or date.

## Script

[ON SCREEN: Close-up. Hands sliding headphones on. Open-plan office behind, rows of desks, no walls.]
Click. You put your headphones on so nobody talks to you.

[ON SCREEN: Pull back. Dozens of identical desks. Every head visible from every angle.]
But your open desk wasn't designed for talking. It was designed in nineteen-oh-six, so you'd never stop being seen.

[ON SCREEN: Title card over the same frame: no text beyond the date "1906".]
And the man who later tried to give you walls... lived to regret it.

[ON SCREEN: Buffalo, New York. A massive brick building. Sign: LARKIN COMPANY.]
Buffalo, New York. The Larkin Company sells soap by mail. Thousands of orders a day. Hundreds of clerks.

[ON SCREEN: Young Frank Lloyd Wright at a drafting table.]
They hire a young architect named Frank Lloyd Wright.

[ON SCREEN: Interior. A tall central court filled with light. Rows of clerks below, balconies above.]
He builds them a cathedral of paperwork. One great open hall. Light pouring from the roof.

[ON SCREEN: Wide shot from a balcony, looking down on every desk at once.]
Beautiful. And from the balconies above, every desk — visible. Every clerk — in sight. Every pause — noticed.

[ON SCREEN: Wright's steel desk with the chair attached to it, swinging out.]
He even designed the furniture. The chairs were bolted to the desks.

[ON SCREEN: The stopwatch from video 1, ticking on a clerk's desk.]
Remember the stopwatch? Around the same time, it walked into the office.

[ON SCREEN: Book cover: "Scientific Office Management", 1917. William Leffingwell.]
William Leffingwell's book put Taylor's methods on the clerk's desk. Desks in rows. Work flowing in a line. The supervisor where he could see it all.

[ON SCREEN: Factory floor dissolving into an office floor. Same rows. Same grid.]
The factory floor had simply moved upstairs.

[ON SCREEN: 1950s Germany. Two brothers over a floor plan covered in curving clusters of desks and plants.]
Then, in the late nineteen-fifties, two brothers in Germany tried to fix it.

[ON SCREEN: Text: "Bürolandschaft — office landscape".]
They called it the office landscape. No rows. No corner offices. Desks in loose clusters, plants between them. Boss and clerk on the same floor.

[ON SCREEN: The clusters slowly being pushed back into straight lines.]
It was meant to be human. Companies kept the open floor... and dropped the rest.

[ON SCREEN: 1968. Robert Propst with a model of movable panels and desks.]
Nineteen sixty-eight. A designer named Robert Propst, at Herman Miller, launches the Action Office.

[ON SCREEN: Propst's original: angled panels, standing desk, room to move.]
His idea: give every worker their own adjustable space. Panels. Privacy. Freedom to move.

[ON SCREEN: The panels snapping into a tight grid of identical boxes. Aerial view.]
Companies took his panels... and packed them into boxes. Smaller. Cheaper. Endless.

[ON SCREEN: A grey cubicle maze stretching to the horizon.]
The cubicle was born.

[ON SCREEN: Old Propst, alone, looking at the maze.]
Near the end of his life, Propst called it "monolithic insanity."

[ON SCREEN: The 2000s. Workers pulling cubicle walls down. Long shared tables. Glass. Beanbags.]
So the walls came down again. This time, they called it collaboration.

[ON SCREEN: Text: Harvard Business School, 2018.]
In twenty eighteen, two Harvard researchers tracked people moving from cubicles into an open office.

[ON SCREEN: Two figures who used to talk now sitting back to back, typing to each other.]
Face-to-face conversation didn't go up. It dropped — by about seventy percent. People emailed and messaged instead.

[ON SCREEN: Headphones on every head across the floor.]
And everyone put their headphones on.

[ON SCREEN: Back to the opening close-up. Your hands. Your headphones.]
A hundred and twenty years. Rows, landscapes, cubicles, open plan. Different shapes. The same balcony.

[ON SCREEN: Slow zoom on the empty balcony from the Larkin building.]
You're not antisocial. You're not difficult.

[ON SCREEN: Headphones settling over the ears. The office noise fades out.]
You're just building the wall they took away.

[ON SCREEN: Black.]
Click.

## Runtime

~350 spoken words at 150 wpm ≈ 2:20. With your slower, dramatic pacing in video 1, expect closer to 2:40-3:00.

## Winning formula

Hook A uses **The Mistake**: it names something the viewer does every day (headphones) and flips
its meaning. It fits because the payoff at the end returns to the same gesture and resolves it.
